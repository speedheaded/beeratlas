Světlý ležák

Czech pale lager · nine taps in ten

This is the beer. If a Prague pub has one tap, this is on it; if it has ten, six of them are this. Learning to read the number in front of it is the single most useful thing a visitor can do with a Czech menu.

The number is not the alcohol

Degrees on a Czech menu are degrees Plato — how much malt sugar went into the wort before fermentation. As a rule of thumb, the alcohol lands near a third of the number.

10° ≈ 3.8 %
Výčepní
The everyday, cheapest pour. Lighter and thinner, made for drinking several. Not a weak version of the 12 — a different beer.
11° ≈ 4.6 %
The middle
Where most regional breweries put their flagship. Fuller than a 10, softer than a 12. Svijany and Kozel live here.
12° ≈ 4.4–5.0 %
Ležák proper
The standard by which Czechs judge a pub. Pilsner Urquell and Budvar are both 12°. Order this first.

Three ways to have it poured

Ask by name and you will be served like a regular, not like a tourist.

Hladinka
The default. Three fingers of dense foam over full beer. Say nothing and this is what arrives.
Šnyt
Less beer, more foam, same glass. For when you want one more but not a whole one. Costs less.
Mlíko
Almost all foam — sweet, creamy, drunk in one go at the end. Not a mistake by the bartender.

Is a 12° stronger than a 10°?

Yes, but less than the numbers suggest — roughly 4.4 % against 3.8 %. The bigger difference is body and depth, not alcohol. A 12° tastes like more beer; a 10° is built for a long afternoon.
