Czech Ten-Degree from Source

A 20-litre recipe for světlé výčepní pivo — the beer Czechs actually drink, and the one their breweries do not export.

Walk into a Prague pub and the tap most people are drinking is not the 12° ležák that leaves the country on pallets. It is the desítka — ten degrees Plato, around 4 % alcohol, and a completely different design brief: full flavour at a strength you can drink four of. Brewed badly it is thin and forgettable, which is why the export market never sees it. Brewed the Czech way it is the most repeatable beer there is.

The beer that does not travel

There is no shortage of Czech pilsner recipes in English. There is almost nothing on the ten-degree, because the breweries that make it well sell it within about forty kilometres of the brewhouse and ship the twelve abroad. If you have drunk Czech beer outside Czechia, you have almost certainly never had this.

That is the whole reason to brew it. Everything below is the same family as a pilsner — same malt, same hop, same yeast — and every number is different.

The target

Original gravity10 °P · 1.040

Grain to glass≈ 30 days

Two degrees of gravity down from a pilsner, and the attenuation goes up, not down — 77 % against 71 %. There is simply less malt to leave behind. That is the trap: run this beer dry and nothing remains. The Czech answer is not to add sugar or specialty grain but to mash warmer, so that what little body there is stays in the glass.

What you are aiming at

Hop character runs 0 to 10, where 0 is the Saaz end — herbal, earthy, faintly floral — and 10 is New World citrus and tropical fruit. A ten-degree sits at 1, exactly where a pilsner does. The strength changes; the hop does not.

The four things that make it Czech

Ten degrees is a design, not a dilution

A Czech ten-degree is not a twelve with water in it. It is mashed warmer — 67–68 °C rather than 65 — so the enzymes make a less fermentable wort and the beer keeps a body its gravity has no right to. Mash a ten-degree at pilsner temperature and you will get exactly the thin, watery beer that gives light lager its reputation.

Water so soft it barely counts

The same rule as the pilsner, and it matters more here: with only 24 IBU to work with, hard water turns what little bitterness there is into something coarse. If your tap water is hard, cut it with distilled or RO water.

Saaz at almost pilsner weight

The instinct is to scale the hops down with the malt. Czech brewers do not — a ten-degree carries roughly 60 % of a pilsner's hop weight for 63 % of its bitterness, because low-gravity wort extracts hops more efficiently. Aroma additions stay almost untouched. It is why a good desítka smells like a beer twice its strength.

Three weeks of cold, not six

Less malt means less to clean up, so a ten-degree matures faster than a ležák. Two to three weeks near 2 °C is enough, and this is the one place the style is genuinely easier than a pilsner. It is also why it is a good first Czech lager: you find out whether you got it right a fortnight sooner.

Recipe · 20 litres into the fermenter

Grain

3.75 kg Czech Pilsner malt, 3–4.9 EBC, and nothing else. Assumes 75 % brewhouse efficiency — scale to yours. Mash at 67–68 °C for 60 minutes. If you decoct, one pull is plenty at this gravity.

IBU by Tinseth at 3.5 % alpha, 20 L, OG 1.040. The lower gravity lifts utilisation about 7 % against a 12° wort, which is already counted above. No dry hop.

Yeast and fermentation

22 g dry lager yeast — two 11 g sachets. The gravity is lower but the pitch is not: 20 L at lager temperature still wants both sachets, and under-pitching shows up faster in a thin beer than a full one. Ferment at 10–12 °C, then drop to 2 °C.

Grain to glass · 30 days

1Day

Mashing

Crushed malt meets hot water and the grain's own enzymes cut its starch into fermentable sugar. The result is wort: sweet, hopless, not yet beer.

67–68 °C for 60 minutes — warmer than a pilsner, deliberately. This single number is most of the difference.

Same day

Boil and hops

Ninety minutes, uncovered. The malt is the same pilsner malt and produces the same DMS, so the boil does not get shorter just because the beer is lighter. Hops go in on the schedule above.

The one step where a ten-degree saves you nothing.

5–8Days

Fermentation

Cool to 10–12 °C and pitch both sachets. Less sugar means a shorter fermentation than a pilsner — five to eight days rather than six to ten.

Stop near 77 % attenuation. There is very little margin here: a few points too far and the beer is water.

14–21Days

Lagering

Rest cold, near 2 °C. Two weeks is the floor, three is comfortable. A ten-degree has less to clean up than a ležák and rewards patience less — the one place this style is kinder than a pilsner.

Finish

Packaging

Unfiltered and unpasteurised, and serve it fresh. A ten-degree is at its best within a month of packaging and does not age gracefully — which is exactly why the breweries that make it well sell it locally.

How solid are these numbers? Less solid than the pilsner sheet, and you should know how. There is no single brewery's published account behind a ten-degree: the target here is the average of the seven ten-degrees in this atlas, and their own figures are largely unverified. The gravity, attenuation and IBU maths are computed and check out; the taste axes are estimates from style and specification, not lab data. Ingredient specifications and prices are the vendor's own.
